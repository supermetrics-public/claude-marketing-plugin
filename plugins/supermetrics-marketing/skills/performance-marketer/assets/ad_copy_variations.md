# Ad copy variations template

Use this when generating new ad copy variations off a winning creative. The mistake to avoid: rewriting the winner so heavily that you lose what made it work. The mistake to also avoid: producing three variations that are just synonym swaps of the original.

The principle: identify what's working, preserve it, vary something else.

## Before writing, name the hook

Look at the winning ad and write down (out loud, to yourself, in a comment — anywhere):

- **What's the emotional hook?** (e.g. fear of missing out, social proof, status, ease, transformation, identity)
- **Who is it talking to?** (specifically — "small business owners worried about cash flow," not "SMBs")
- **What's the call to action doing?** ("Start free trial" is low-commitment; "Book a demo" is sales-qualified; "Download the report" is top-of-funnel education)
- **What's the format constraint?** (Facebook primary text vs. LinkedIn intro vs. Google headline — each has different length and tone norms)

You can't write good variations without naming these. Variations should change one of the latter three while keeping the first one (the hook) intact.

## The three variations to produce

### Variation 1: Different CTA, same hook, same audience

The hook still lands; the action changes. Useful when the winning ad's CTA might be filtering out otherwise-good leads (e.g. "Buy now" → "See pricing" can pull in more research-stage prospects).

**Winning ad CTA:** [original]
**New CTA:** [new]
**Body:**
[Write 1-3 variations of the body, keeping the hook identical to the winner but shaped around the new CTA.]

### Variation 2: Same hook, slightly different audience tilt

The hook works; sharpen who it's talking to. Useful when the winning ad is broad and there's evidence (or hypothesis) that one audience segment is over-indexing.

**Original audience framing:** [from the winner]
**Tilted to:** [specific segment]
**Body:**
[1-3 versions. Keep the emotional payoff identical. What changes is who the ad addresses by name, by role, by situation.]

### Variation 3: Same hook, different proof point

The hook works; back it with different evidence. Useful when the original used a testimonial and you have data; or used a stat and you have a customer story. The argument is the same, the substantiation differs.

**Original proof:** [stat / testimonial / before-after / etc.]
**New proof:** [the alternative]
**Body:**
[1-3 versions.]

## What to avoid

- **Synonym swaps.** "Boost your business" → "Grow your business" → "Scale your business" is not three variations; it's the same ad three times.
- **Different hooks.** If the winner works on FOMO and you write a variation on social proof, you're not testing a variation — you're starting a new test. Make it explicit if that's what you're doing.
- **Ignoring format constraints.** Facebook lets you write long; Google search RSA headlines max at 30 characters. The variation has to fit the format it's running in.
- **Writing for the brand, not the buyer.** "Industry-leading solutions" never beat a sentence that sounded like one person talking to another.
